# Question Generation Skill

Frameworks for generating the three types of facilitator preparation materials — question trees, insight triggers, and context cards — that arm the BELIEF facilitation team with world-class preparation.

## Three Types of Preparation Materials

### 1. Question Trees

Branching conversation paths that anticipate where a discussion might go. If the client answers X, follow up with Y. If they answer Z, pivot to W.

**Structure:**
```
PRIMARY QUESTION: [The opening question]
├── If they emphasize [theme A]:
│   ├── FOLLOW-UP: [Deepen on theme A]
│   └── PROBE: [Challenge or test theme A]
├── If they emphasize [theme B]:
│   ├── FOLLOW-UP: [Explore theme B implications]
│   └── PIVOT: [Connect theme B to positioning]
├── If they deflect or give a generic answer:
│   ├── REFRAME: [Ask the same thing differently]
│   └── CONTRAST: [Offer a specific scenario to react to]
└── If they disagree with the premise:
    ├── EXPLORE: [Why do they see it differently?]
    └── SIGNAL: [Their disagreement IS the insight — note it]
```

**Rules for good question trees:**
- Never more than 3 levels deep — this is a guide, not a script
- Each branch should feel natural, not mechanical
- Include at least one "deflection recovery" path — what to do when they give a non-answer
- The tree should converge toward a positioning insight, even from different starting branches

### 2. Insight Triggers

Questions designed to surface specific tensions, contradictions, or hidden truths that the research has already identified. The question is the vehicle for the client to discover something about themselves.

**Structure:**
```
INSIGHT TRIGGER: [The question to ask]
TENSION: [The contradiction or gap this is designed to surface]
EVIDENCE: [What in the research suggests this tension exists]
EXPECTED RESPONSE: [What they're likely to say]
THE REAL INSIGHT: [What their response actually reveals]
IF THEY SURPRISE US: [What to do if they respond unexpectedly]
```

**Where insight triggers come from:**
- **Contradictions in documents**: homework says one thing, materials show another
- **Say-do gaps**: stated values don't match observed behavior
- **Internal disagreements**: different documents from different people tell different stories
- **Market perception gaps**: how the company presents itself vs. how the market sees it
- **Competitive blind spots**: competitors they ignore or dismiss without good reason
- **Unspoken assumptions**: things taken for granted that may not be true

**Rules for good insight triggers:**
- Never accusatory — frame as curiosity, not confrontation
- The client should feel like they're discovering the insight, not being told
- The best triggers feel like simple questions but unlock complex realizations
- Always have a plan for the unexpected response — it might be more valuable than the expected one

### 3. Context Cards

Background briefings for the facilitator explaining WHY each question matters, what signal to listen for in the answer, and what it tells us about brand positioning.

**Structure:**
```
CONTEXT CARD: [Question or trigger this card supports]
WHY THIS MATTERS: [What positioning decision this informs]
LISTEN FOR: [Specific words, phrases, emotional reactions to note]
WHAT IT TELLS US: [How different answers map to positioning directions]
RED FLAGS: [Responses that indicate deeper issues to address]
CONNECTION TO: [How this links to other questions or session stages]
```

**Rules for good context cards:**
- Keep them scannable — the facilitator reads them in moments between questions, not in advance
- Focus on what to LISTEN FOR, not what to SAY — the facilitator's words should be spontaneous
- Link cards to positioning outcomes — "if they say X, it suggests positioning direction Y"
- Note emotional signals, not just verbal content — enthusiasm, hesitation, disagreement

## The Socratic Method in Brand Discovery

### Core Principles

The Socratic method in BELIEF is not about proving the facilitator right. It's about helping the leadership team articulate what they already know but haven't said.

**Five Socratic moves for brand discovery:**

1. **Clarifying questions** — "What do you mean when you say [their word]?"
   - Use when: they use jargon, buzzwords, or vague language
   - Why: forces precision and often reveals that team members have different definitions

2. **Probing assumptions** — "What makes you confident that [stated belief]?"
   - Use when: they state something as obvious that may not be
   - Why: surfaces the evidence (or lack thereof) behind brand assumptions

3. **Exploring implications** — "If that's true, then what does that mean for [downstream decision]?"
   - Use when: they've made a statement but haven't thought through consequences
   - Why: connects values and beliefs to brand actions and positioning

4. **Considering alternatives** — "What would someone who disagrees say?"
   - Use when: the team is too comfortable with a position
   - Why: stress-tests brand positions and reveals conviction levels

5. **Questioning the question** — "Is that the right question to be asking?"
   - Use when: the discussion is going in circles or addressing symptoms
   - Why: reframes the conversation toward root causes and fundamental identity

### The Art of the Follow-Up

The first answer is rarely the real answer. The facilitator's job is to go deeper:

- **If they give a corporate answer**: "That's what your website says. What would you say at a dinner party?"
- **If they all agree too quickly**: "I'm surprised there's no disagreement here. [Name], you looked like you wanted to add something."
- **If they're stuck**: "Let me make it easier — what are you definitely NOT?"
- **If they give a list**: "If you had to pick just one of those, which one?"
- **If two people disagree**: "This disagreement is valuable. Let's stay with it for a moment."

### 2nd-Order Thinking in Question Design

A first-order question asks about the topic directly. A second-order question asks about the consequences, implications, and downstream effects.

**First-order**: "What's your biggest competitive advantage?"
**Second-order**: "If a competitor copied your biggest advantage tomorrow, what would still make customers choose you?"

**First-order**: "Who is your target customer?"
**Second-order**: "If your ideal customer were describing you to a friend, what would they say — and what do you wish they'd say instead?"

**First-order**: "What are your company values?"
**Second-order**: "Tell me about a time you turned down revenue because it conflicted with a value."

**Design pattern for 2nd-order questions:**
1. Start with the obvious question (what they expect)
2. Accept their answer (don't challenge it yet)
3. Push to the consequence layer: "Given that, what does that mean for..."
4. Push to the commitment layer: "So if that's true, you'd be willing to..."
5. Push to the identity layer: "What does that say about who you are?"

This progression — fact → consequence → commitment → identity — is the engine of brand discovery.

## Mode-Specific Question Emphasis

### new-brand Questions

Focus on origin, intent, and aspiration:
- "Why does this company exist? Not what it does — why does it need to exist?"
- "What problem were you experiencing that made you think 'someone needs to solve this'?"
- "If you succeed completely in 10 years, what's different about the world?"
- "Who is the person you most want to serve, and what do they wish existed?"

### rebrand-tweak Questions

Focus on evolution and perception:
- "What about the current brand still feels right?"
- "When people misunderstand what you do, what do they think you are?"
- "What has changed — in your market, your capabilities, or your ambitions — since the brand was last defined?"
- "If you could change one thing about how customers perceive you, what would it be?"

### rebrand-pivot Questions

Focus on transformation and departure:
- "What happened that made you realize the current brand no longer fits?"
- "What from the old brand are you proud of, and what do you want to leave behind?"
- "How do you want your longest-standing customers to feel about the change?"
- "If you met someone who only knew the old brand, what would surprise them most about who you are now?"

## Playbook Assembly

When assembling the Facilitator's Playbook, organize materials by session stage:

**Stage 1 (Discovery)**: Questions that validate and calibrate research findings. Insight triggers based on research contradictions. Questions that surface the team's unfiltered perspective.

**Stage 2 (Experience)**: Questions about competitors and market position. Insight triggers based on competitive blind spots. Questions that reveal where the team sees opportunity.

**Stage 3 (Interpret)**: Questions that force choices and prioritization. Insight triggers based on say-do gaps. Questions that move from abstract values to concrete commitments.

**Stage 4 (Express)**: Questions about adoption and change management. Questions that test conviction and durability of decisions. Forward-looking questions that connect the session to execution.

For each stage, include:
- 3-5 primary questions (with question trees)
- 2-3 insight triggers (with context cards)
- A "what to listen for" summary
- A "wildcards" section for unexpected threads
