# Research Methodology Skill

Frameworks and guidance for conducting the seven research areas that inform BELIEF brand discovery sessions. This research runs independently of client-provided materials (the T-3 parallel track) and should be started as early as possible.

## Required Inputs

Confirm these before you start:

- **Client name and website** — the anchor for all seven areas
- **Engagement mode** — new-brand / rebrand-tweak / rebrand-pivot; it drives the emphasis shifts below. If the mode is unknown, ask.
- **Client homework materials** — if they are missing, proceed on public sources and flag the gap in each affected area's output

This research requires web search access.

## The Seven Research Areas

### 1. Company Overview

**What to find:** History, founding story, leadership team backgrounds, company size and stage, funding history, notable milestones, culture signals, public statements by leadership.

**Where to look:**
- Company website (About, Team, Press pages)
- LinkedIn company page and leadership profiles
- Crunchbase, PitchBook (funding, stage)
- News archives (founding stories, milestone coverage)
- Glassdoor (culture signals, leadership reviews)
- Public interviews, podcasts, or conference talks by leadership

**What signals matter:**
- Gap between how leaders describe the company vs. how the website presents it
- Hiring patterns that reveal strategic direction
- Cultural language and values that appear organically vs. formally stated

### 2. Current Brand Expression

**What to find:** How the company currently presents itself across all touchpoints — messaging, visual identity, tone of voice, consistency.

**Where to look:**
- Website: homepage headline, tagline, value proposition, About page language
- Social media profiles: bios, post style, visual consistency
- Marketing materials: brochures, pitch decks (if available from client materials)
- Email communications (if samples provided)
- Any existing brand guidelines or style documents

**What signals matter:**
- Consistency (or inconsistency) across channels
- Sophistication level of current brand expression
- Gap between the company's capabilities and how they present them
- Dated or misaligned visual/verbal identity

### 3. Competitive Landscape

**What to find:** Who competes for the same customers, how competitors position themselves, where differentiation opportunities exist.

**Where to look:**
- Direct competitor websites (messaging, positioning, value propositions)
- Industry directories and market maps
- Review sites (G2, Capterra, TrustRadius for B2B; Yelp, Google Reviews for B2C)
- Industry analyst reports and roundups
- Search results for the client's core keywords
- Social media presence and engagement of competitors

**What signals matter:**
- How competitors describe the category and the problem they solve
- Claimed differentiators — are they real or generic?
- Visual and verbal positioning patterns — what's the industry default?
- Customer complaints about competitors — unmet needs are positioning opportunities

### 4. Target Audience Signals

**What to find:** Who the client's customers are, what they care about, how they make decisions, what unmet needs exist.

**Where to look:**
- Client-provided customer personas or descriptions (from homework)
- Industry forums, Reddit, community discussions
- Customer reviews of the client and competitors
- Social media conversations in the client's space
- Industry publications that the target audience reads
- Job postings from companies in the target audience (reveals priorities)

**What signals matter:**
- Language customers use to describe their problems (vs. how companies describe solutions)
- Decision-making factors and priorities
- Emotional drivers (what keeps them up at night)
- Unmet needs that no one addresses well

### 5. Market & Industry Trends

**What to find:** Macro trends affecting the client's market — technology shifts, regulatory changes, demographic changes, economic factors.

**Where to look:**
- Industry publications and trade journals
- Market research reports (excerpts and summaries)
- News coverage of the industry
- Conference themes and keynote topics
- Regulatory and government announcements
- Technology trend reports

**What signals matter:**
- Trends that create tailwinds or headwinds for the client's positioning
- Shifts that make current competitor positioning obsolete
- Emerging customer expectations driven by market changes
- Regulatory changes that create differentiation opportunities

### 6. White Space Analysis

**What to find:** Positioning opportunities — where competitors aren't, what audiences need that nobody delivers, category-defining opportunities.

**Where to look:** This is synthesized from the previous five areas rather than researched independently.

**How to synthesize:**
- Map competitors on a 2x2 positioning grid using the two most important dimensions
- Identify underserved audience segments from audience research
- Find unaddressed customer needs from reviews and community discussions
- Look for positioning language that no competitor owns
- Consider category-creation opportunities — is there a way to reframe the space?

**What signals matter:**
- Gaps in the positioning map where no competitor sits
- Customer needs expressed in reviews/forums that no competitor addresses
- Emerging trends that create new positioning opportunities
- Intersection points where the client's unique capabilities meet unmet needs

### 7. Past Campaigns & Creative

**What to find:** The client's marketing history — what they've tried, what worked, what didn't, creative direction evolution.

**Where to look:**
- Client-provided materials and case studies
- Wayback Machine (website evolution over time)
- Social media post history
- Press coverage of past campaigns
- Ad libraries (Meta, Google Ads Transparency)
- Awards or recognition for past work

**What signals matter:**
- Evolution of messaging over time — is the direction consistent or scattered?
- Campaigns that performed well — what resonated with the audience?
- Campaigns that fell flat — why? (misaligned messaging, wrong audience, bad timing?)
- Creative style evolution — what visual direction has the brand been moving toward?

## Mode-Specific Research Emphasis

### new-brand

For companies building a brand from scratch, research emphasis shifts to:
- **Company Overview**: Deep dive on founder intent and vision — what drove them to start this?
- **Competitive Landscape**: Extra thorough — with no existing brand, differentiation must be intentional from day one
- **White Space**: This is the critical section — where can they plant a flag that nobody else owns?
- **Target Audience**: Focus on unmet needs — what does the market want that they're uniquely positioned to deliver?
- De-emphasize: Current Brand Expression (there isn't one) and Past Campaigns (there aren't any)

### rebrand-tweak

For brands refining their existing identity, research emphasis shifts to:
- **Current Brand Expression**: Deep audit of what exists — what's working, what's dated, what's misaligned
- **Competitive Landscape**: Focus on how the market has evolved since the brand was last refreshed
- **Target Audience**: Focus on perception gaps — how do customers see the brand vs. how the company sees itself?
- **Market Trends**: What has changed in the market that makes the current brand feel off?
- Maintain: All areas at normal depth

### rebrand-pivot

For brands undergoing transformation, research emphasis shifts to:
- **Company Overview**: What changed? New leadership, new strategy, new market, acquisition?
- **Current Brand Expression**: Document what exists thoroughly — the team needs to understand what they're leaving behind
- **Competitive Landscape**: Where does the new positioning need to land? What space is available?
- **Market Trends**: What market shift is driving the pivot? Is the timing right?
- **Past Campaigns**: Important to understand what the brand has been known for, since the pivot needs to overcome existing associations
- **White Space**: Critical — the pivot needs a clear destination, not just a departure

## Research Output Standards

For each area, research output should include:

1. **Key Findings** — 3-5 bullet points of the most important discoveries
2. **Supporting Evidence** — specific sources, quotes, data points
3. **Implications for Brand Discovery** — what this means for the BELIEF session
4. **Questions This Raises** — new questions generated by the research
5. **Confidence Level** — high/medium/low based on source quality and consistency

Flag any findings that contradict the client's homework or self-description — these are valuable insight triggers for the Facilitator's Playbook.

## Research Quality Standards

- Always note the date of research — competitive intelligence has a short shelf life
- Distinguish between facts (verifiable), signals (inferred from evidence), and speculation (interpretation)
- Include source references so findings can be verified
- When web search returns limited results, note the gap rather than inventing findings
- Cross-reference findings across multiple sources when possible
- Prioritize recency — information from the last 12 months is most valuable

## Deliverable

Write the seven-area report to the engagement folder as `research/we-marketing-research-YYYY-MM-DD.md`, one section per area in the Output Standards format above.

Close by:

- Linking the report file
- Listing the high-confidence findings
- Calling out contradictions flagged for the Facilitator's Playbook

## Approval Gates

- **Autonomous:** all seven research areas — public-source research, synthesis, and writing the report to the engagement folder need no sign-off.
- **Ask first:** paying for reports or data sources; sharing findings outside the engagement folder.
- **Never:** contact the client, competitors, or customers directly.
